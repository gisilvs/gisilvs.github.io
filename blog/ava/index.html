Ava: Text-to-Synth

By Gianluigi Silvestri

Preamble

A few years ago, I got into music production as a hobby. I spent quite some time learning about sound design, although I never became particularly good at it. My usual workflow was something like this: I had a sound in mind, tried to recreate it on my favorite synthesizer, failed, and started scrolling endlessly through presets. I would find plenty of interesting sounds, but rarely the one I was looking for. Eventually, I would get distracted, forget my original idea, and give up.

Around the same time, I became curious about how synthesizers actually worked. I started reading The Audio Programmer's book on building synthesizer plugins, and realized that much of a synthesizer is essentially a sequence of mathematical operations on audio signals, many of which can be made differentiable.

That got me thinking: what if I implemented a synthesizer in PyTorch, and used backpropagation to learn its parameters?

The original idea was sound-to-synth: given an audio sample, a neural network would predict the synthesizer parameters needed to reproduce it. By passing these parameters through a differentiable synth and comparing the resulting audio with the original, we could train the network end-to-end, using the synthesizer itself as the decoder.

But then I realized this wasn't quite the problem I wanted to solve. How often do you have an audio sample of the exact sound you're imagining? Most of the time, you can at least try to describe it: a warm, slightly detuned pad with a slow attack, or a bright, punchy pluck. And even if the result isn't perfect, having a reasonably close preset to start tweaking would already make sound design much easier.

So the idea evolved into text-to-synth. I initially considered using a joint audio-text embedding model like CLAP, but eventually decided to take advantage of pretrained text-to-audio generative models to create paired text and audio data, and learn to map the resulting sounds to synthesizer parameters.

The result is Ava: a simple subtractive synthesizer that lets you generate presets from natural language descriptions, while still being able to play and tweak them like in any regular synth.

In this post, I'll walk through the synthesizer implementation, the training experiments, what worked (and what didn't), and how everything came together in a working plugin. Most of the project was developed on my MacBook M1 Pro, with a few hours of A100 GPU time for the more expensive experiments, and plenty of help from the open-source community.

And why Ava? It's named after the robot protagonist of Ex Machina, the movie that inspired me to study AI many years ago.

The Synth

Let's start with the synthesizer itself. For this project, I wanted something relatively simple, but still expressive enough to generate a good variety of sounds. So I went with a subtractive synthesizer.

For those unfamiliar with sound synthesis, a subtractive synthesizer starts by generating one or more waveforms, typically rich in harmonics, and then shapes the resulting sound using filters, envelopes, and other modulations. It's one of the most common approaches to sound synthesis, and a good starting point for this project.

Ava follows a fairly standard architecture, illustrated in the diagram below.

The synth has two oscillators, each supporting four waveforms: sine, triangle, square, and sawtooth. Their outputs are combined using a mixing parameter, and the second oscillator can be detuned relative to the first to create richer sounds. White noise can also be blended into the mixed signal before it passes through a resonant low-pass filter, whose cutoff frequency can be modulated by a low-frequency oscillator (LFO). The signal then passes through drive, followed by an ADSR amplitude envelope and output gain.

This gives us a relatively small set of parameters to predict:

  • Oscillators: waveform selection for each oscillator, mix, and detuning.
  • Noise: white-noise mix.
  • Filter: cutoff frequency and resonance.
  • LFO: modulation rate and depth.
  • Envelope: attack, decay, sustain, and release.
  • Output: drive and gain.

One important design decision was to exclude pitch and velocity from the predicted parameters. Unlike the other parameters, which define a synth preset, pitch and velocity are generally determined by the incoming MIDI notes. Besides, estimating pitch from audio is already a challenging problem on its own, and I didn't want the model to have to solve both problems simultaneously.

Instead, the synthesizer receives pitch, velocity, and note-on/note-off events as separate MIDI inputs, while the neural network only predicts the preset parameters. This simplifies the learning problem, although it also means that we need to know the MIDI information associated with each training sample. We'll see later how I dealt with that.

The resulting synth is intentionally quite limited compared to commercial synthesizers. There are no effects like reverb or chorus, no complex modulation matrix, and only two oscillators. But that's also part of the experiment: how much can we do with a relatively simple synthesis engine, and how well can a neural network learn to control it?

Deep Dive: Building a Differentiable Synthesizer in PyTorchOptional · open to read

The main idea behind this project is that we want to use a synthesizer as a differentiable decoder. Given a set of parameters \theta and MIDI information m, the synthesizer produces an audio waveform:

\hat{x} = S(\theta, m)

We can then compare this waveform with target audio and backpropagate the loss through the synthesizer. The distinction is that \theta is an input to the synth, just like the MIDI information. The synthesizer itself is a fixed collection of signal-processing operations; it has no internal neural-network weights to learn.

For a single sound, we can make \theta a set of learnable tensors and optimize those values directly until the rendered audio matches the target. Alternatively, we can train a neural network to predict \theta from an audio representation. In that case, the network learns a mapping that we can reuse across many sounds, rather than solving a new optimization problem for each one. This is usually called amortized inference.

In both cases, gradients pass through the same synthesizer. What changes is whether we update the preset values themselves or the weights of the network that predicts them.

Most of these operations are relatively straightforward to differentiate. Others, particularly discrete waveform selection and recursive filtering, require a bit more care.

Oscillators and waveform selection

Let's start with the oscillators. An oscillator generates a periodic waveform at a given frequency, determined by the incoming MIDI pitch. Ava supports four waveforms: sine, triangle, square, and sawtooth.

To generate audio, we evaluate a waveform at a sequence of evenly spaced times. In the equation below, t is time in seconds since note-on, and f is frequency in cycles per second (hertz):

x(t) = \sin(2\pi ft)

A sample rate of 24 kHz means taking 24,000 samples per second, so adjacent times are 1/24000 seconds apart. If f_s is the sample rate and N is the number of samples, the time vector is:

t_n = \frac{n}{f_s}, \qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1

The synth builds that vector once for the clip. Here, duration is in seconds and self.sample_rate is the number of samples per second:

From synth/synth.py, with explanatory comments

# Sample indices 0, 1, ..., N-1 become times in seconds after division.
time = torch.arange(round(duration * self.sample_rate),
                    device=freq.device, dtype=torch.float32) / self.sample_rate
# Match the frequency tensor's dtype; time still has shape [samples].
time = time.to(freq)

Multiplying each time by freq tells us how many cycles have elapsed. Multiplying again by 2\pi converts cycles to an angle in radians. Finally, sine maps that angle to an amplitude between −1 and 1. At 200 Hz and 24 kHz, this takes 120 samples per cycle.

From synth/oscillators/sine.py, with explanatory comments

def __call__(self, freq, time):
    # freq: [batch, 1]; time: [samples].
    # Broadcasting evaluates every time point at each batch element's frequency.
    # 2*pi*freq*time is the phase in radians; initial phase is zero.
    return torch.sin(2 * torch.pi * freq * time)  # [batch, samples]

Every batch element can therefore have its own pitch, while sharing the same sampling times. For now there is no phase-offset control: all oscillators start at phase zero.

Square, triangle and sawtooth are built by adding sine waves at multiples of the fundamental frequency. These extra components are called harmonics. A square wave uses only odd harmonics: f,3f,5f,\ldots, with each amplitude divided by its harmonic number:

x_{\mathrm{square}}(t) \approx \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin(2\pi ft)+\frac{\sin(2\pi 3ft)}{3}+\frac{\sin(2\pi 5ft)}{5}+\cdots\right]

We start with the fundamental sine wave. Adding a smaller sine at three times its frequency begins to flatten the top and bottom. Further odd harmonics make the transitions steeper. The second component we add is therefore the third harmonic; the second harmonic, 2f, is absent.

Ava includes odd harmonics up to the 31st for square and triangle, and every harmonic up to the 31st for sawtooth. It also excludes any harmonic at or above half the sample rate, the Nyquist frequency. These are finite approximations: the small ripples in the square wave come from stopping the harmonic sum.

The square-wave code evaluates the harmonics together. Below, B is the batch size, N is the number of audio samples, and H = 16 is the number of odd harmonics from 1 to 31. Extra dimensions let PyTorch combine each frequency, harmonic and time without a Python loop:

From synth/oscillators/square.py, with explanatory comments

# Odd harmonic numbers: 1, 3, ..., 31. Initially [H].
harmonics = torch.arange(1, 32, 2, device=freq.device, dtype=freq.dtype)
harmonics = harmonics.view(1, -1, 1)  # [1, H, 1]
freq = freq.unsqueeze(1)             # [B, 1, 1]
time = time.unsqueeze(-2)            # [1, N], broadcast as [1, 1, N]
allowed = freq * harmonics < self.sample_rate / 2  # [B, H, 1]
# One sine wave per harmonic, evaluated at every sample time.
waves = torch.sin(2 * torch.pi * freq * harmonics * time)  # [B, H, N]
# Mask, weight by 1/harmonic, then sum H to produce [B, N].
return 4 / torch.pi * (allowed * waves / harmonics).sum(dim=1)

Before the final sum, waves contains a separate sine wave for every batch element and harmonic. Dividing by harmonics makes higher harmonics quieter; allowed removes those above the frequency limit. Summing dimension 1 combines the harmonics into one audio signal per batch element.

Compare the four oscillator waveforms

So far, pitch has come from MIDI and phase has been fixed at zero. We have not yet introduced a learnable preset control. The first one we want to learn is which oscillator waveform to use.

Waveform selection is a discrete operation: each oscillator must choose one of four possible waveforms. Unfortunately, a hard selection, such as taking the argmax of a neural network's output, is not differentiable.

One solution is to replace the hard selection with a softmax-weighted combination of all four waveforms:

x(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{4}p_k x_k(t), \qquad p_k=\operatorname{softmax}(z)_k

where z represents the predicted logits and x_k(t) the individual waveforms.

For soft selection, the caller turns four logits into four probabilities using softmax, then passes them to the oscillator bank as selection. Each oscillator returns [batch, samples] audio. We stack those signals along a new axis, multiply each one by its probability, and sum over the four choices:

From synth/oscillators/bank.py

# Keep the four candidate waveforms on a separate axis: [batch, 4, samples].
waves = torch.stack([osc(freq, time) for osc in self.oscillators], dim=1)
# selection: [batch, 4] -> [batch, 4, 1], sharing each weight across time.
# Sum the waveform axis to return [batch, samples].
return (selection.unsqueeze(-1) * waves).sum(dim=1)

The bank's order is sine, square, sawtooth, triangle. Weights of [1, 0, 0, 0] therefore give a sine wave, while [0.5, 0.5, 0, 0] blend sine and square equally. This choice belongs to a single oscillator; the separate mix control later combines oscillator A with oscillator B.

This makes waveform selection differentiable, but introduces a mismatch: during training, the oscillator can mix multiple waveforms, whereas in the final plugin we want to select a single waveform.

An alternative is the straight-through estimator (STE), which uses a hard selection during the forward pass but approximates its gradients with those of the soft selection.

For one oscillator, the fitting code expresses this as:

# Turn four logits into probabilities, then make a one-hot hard choice.
probability = raw.softmax(dim=-1)
hard = torch.nn.functional.one_hot(probability.argmax(-1), 4).to(probability)
# The bracket is zero in the forward pass, but carries softmax gradients.
controls[name] = hard + (probability - probability.detach())

Here, raw contains the four logits. detach() keeps a tensor's values but removes its connection to the gradient computation. Subtracting the detached probabilities therefore changes nothing in the forward pass: the synth receives only hard. During backpropagation, however, gradients still flow through the non-detached probabilities. This is a surrogate gradient, not the derivative of the hard choice.

I experimented with both approaches, and found that soft selection was generally more stable, while the straight-through estimator made optimization considerably less reliable. We'll come back to this in the parameter recovery experiments.

Detuning and mixing

Both oscillators begin with the MIDI note's frequency, but we can shift the second one slightly. Detuning is measured in cents: there are 100 cents in a semitone and 1,200 in an octave. Since moving up one octave doubles frequency, a shift of c cents multiplies frequency by 2^{c/1200}:

f_B = f_A\,2^{c/1200}

At zero cents the frequencies are identical. At +12 cents, a 200 Hz note becomes approximately 201.39 Hz. When we mix two close frequencies, their interference creates slow beating and a sense of movement, without changing the MIDI note being played.

The code first renders oscillator B at its shifted frequency, then crossfades between A and B. A mix of zero uses only A, one uses only B, and 0.5 gives equal contributions:

From synth/synth.py, with explanatory comments

# Convert the cents offset to a frequency ratio for oscillator B.
freq_b = freq * 2 ** (detune_cents / 1200)
wave_b = self.oscillators(freq_b, time, oscillator_b)
# wave is oscillator A here; mix controls their relative contribution.
wave = (1 - mix) * wave + mix * wave_b

After mixing the oscillators, a separate noise mix blends in white noise before the filter.

ADSR envelope

The oscillators give us a repeating waveform. An ADSR envelope gives the note its amplitude shape: attack raises the level from zero to one, decay lowers it toward sustain, sustain holds that level while the note is pressed, and release brings it back to zero after note-off. We multiply the audio by this envelope, sample by sample.

Attack, decay and release are durations in seconds; sustain is a level between zero and one. MIDI timing supplies when the note starts and stops. We optimize the envelope controls, while keeping those MIDI events as known inputs.

To implement the transitions, we first need a curve that maps progress through a stage to a level between zero and one. For example, time / attack is zero at note-on and one at the end of attack. The helper below bends that progress into a normalized exponential curve:

From synth/envelopes/adsr.py, with explanatory comments

def curve(self, progress):
    # Before a stage starts, progress is 0; after it ends, progress is 1.
    progress = progress.clamp(0, 1)
    # self.curvature = 4.0. Normalize the exponential to run from 0 to 1.
    curve = -torch.expm1(-self.curvature * progress) / -math.expm1(-self.curvature)
    # Make completed transitions exactly 1, including in finite precision.
    return torch.where(progress >= 1, torch.ones_like(curve), curve)

expm1(x) computes e^x-1 accurately even near zero. The expression above is (1-e^{-4p})/(1-e^{-4}), where p is clamped progress. The denominator makes the endpoint one. The fixed curvature of 4 determines the bend: halfway through the stage, this curve has reached about 88% of its endpoint.

We can now use that same curve for both attack and decay. During attack, it directly gives the amplitude. After attack, we measure progress through decay and use the curve to remove a fraction of the difference between one and sustain:

From synth/envelopes/adsr.py, with explanatory comments

def held_level(self, time, attack, decay, sustain):
    attack_level = self.curve(time / attack)  # Progress through attack.
    # Decay begins at time = attack, and drops by (1 - sustain).
    decay_level = 1 - (1 - sustain) * self.curve((time - attack) / decay)
    return torch.where(time < attack, attack_level, decay_level)

At the start of decay, its progress is zero, so the level is still one. At its end, the curve reaches one and the level becomes sustain. Because progress stays clamped at one afterwards, the same expression keeps the note at its sustain level until note-off.

Release needs one more detail: the note might stop during attack or decay. We first evaluate the held-note envelope at the exact note-off time, then fade from that level to zero. This avoids jumping to the sustain level when the note is released early:

From synth/envelopes/adsr.py, with explanatory comments

# Amplitude while the key is held, evaluated at every audio sample.
held = self.held_level(time, attack, decay, sustain)
# Evaluate the same function at note-off, even if attack/decay is unfinished.
level_at_release = self.held_level(note_press_duration, attack, decay, sustain)
# Fade that level to zero over the release duration.
tail = level_at_release * (1 - self.curve((time - note_press_duration) / release))
return torch.where(time < note_press_duration, held, tail)

The release progress is (time - note_press_duration) / release. Its curve rises from zero to one, so 1 - curve falls from one to zero. Multiplying by level_at_release gives a tail that starts exactly where the held note ended. The final torch.where uses the held envelope before note-off and this tail afterwards.

In these snippets, the controls have shape [batch, 1] and time has shape [samples], producing an envelope of shape [batch, samples]. Clip duration is separate from note duration: rendering six seconds does not mean holding the key for six seconds, and a clip can end before a long release has finished.

Low-pass filter and LFO modulation

The filter was probably the most interesting component to implement.

Ava uses a second-order resonant low-pass filter, also known as a biquad filter. It attenuates frequencies above a specified cutoff, while the resonance parameter controls the emphasis around that cutoff.

A biquad filter can be expressed using the following recurrence:

\begin{aligned} y[n] ={}& b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1] + b_2 x[n-2]\\ &- a_1 y[n-1] - a_2 y[n-2] \end{aligned}

The coefficients depend on the cutoff frequency and resonance Q. For a modulated filter, each coefficient is time-dependent; the recurrence uses the coefficient values for the current sample.

Unlike these oscillators and envelopes, this biquad is recursive: each output sample depends not only on the input, but also on previous output samples. This makes it harder to implement efficiently using standard vectorized PyTorch operations.

Things become even more complicated when we introduce the LFO.

The LFO periodically modulates the filter cutoff, creating movement in the sound. In Ava, the modulation follows a sinusoidal curve, with its rate and depth controlled by two additional parameters. The depth is expressed in octaves, so the cutoff varies proportionally rather than by a fixed number of hertz:

f_c(t) = f_{\text{base}}\, 2^{d\sin(2\pi f_{\text{LFO}}t)}

Here, the base cutoff is the played note's frequency shifted by cutoff_octaves. Both the base offset and LFO depth are measured in octaves:

update_times = time[::self.lfo_block_size]
offset = offset + self.lfo(update_times, rate, depth)
cutoff_hz = freq.double() * 2 ** offset

The LFO itself returns depth * torch.sin(2 * torch.pi * rate * time). In the default configuration, coefficients update at 200 Hz, not at every audio sample. At 24 kHz, each set lasts 120 samples. Setting the control rate to the audio sample rate gives sample-wise updates. The sinusoid is smooth, but the default filter coefficients are held between updates.

These snippets use physical controls. The synth maps normalized Q outputs logarithmically to 0.5–10, and LFO rate to 0.5–10 Hz. At 24 kHz, the supported cutoff range is 20 Hz–10 kHz; decoded LFO depth is limited to two octaves or the available cutoff headroom, whichever is smaller.

Fortunately, I didn't have to implement differentiable recursive filtering from scratch. I used TorchLPC, which provides an efficient differentiable implementation of recursive linear filtering. This allowed me to compute gradients through the filter even with time-varying coefficients.

The coefficients use the standard low-pass biquad equations. They are divided by the first denominator coefficient, so the recurrence has an implicit a_0=1:

From synth/filters/lowpass.py

omega = 2 * torch.pi * cutoff_hz / self.sample_rate
cosine = torch.cos(omega)
alpha = torch.sin(omega) / (2 * q)
a = torch.cat([1 + alpha, -2 * cosine, 1 - alpha], dim=-1)
b = torch.cat([(1 - cosine) / 2, 1 - cosine, (1 - cosine) / 2], dim=-1)
# Normalize so the first denominator coefficient is 1.
return a / a[:, :1], b / a[:, :1]

For the time-varying path, the coefficient sets are repeated across their update intervals. In the following excerpt, a and b already have shape [batch, samples, 3], and x is the input audio. The feedforward part is vectorized; TorchLPC handles the recursive denominator:

From synth/filters/lowpass.py

# Current and previous input samples, with zero history before the clip.
padded = torch.nn.functional.pad(x, (2, 0))
numerator = b[..., 0] * x + b[..., 1] * padded[:, 1:-1] + b[..., 2] * padded[:, :-2]
# One recursive pass preserves history across every coefficient update.
filtered = sample_wise_lpc(numerator.contiguous(), a[..., 1:].contiguous())

The leading denominator coefficient is omitted because it is one. The two preceding input samples are zero before the clip, and TorchLPC starts with zero recursive history. Importantly, there is one recursive pass across the whole clip: coefficient updates do not reset the filter state.

The filter uses float64 internally to improve numerical stability at low cutoffs and high resonance, then casts its output back to the audio dtype. Without modulation, it instead uses torchaudio.functional.lfilter. Both paths preserve gradients through the audio and filter controls; the modulated path also connects them to LFO rate and depth.

Drive, gain, and putting everything together

The remaining components are relatively simple. Drive introduces nonlinear distortion, adding harmonics to the signal, while gain controls the final output level.

Drive uses normalized hyperbolic-tangent saturation:

y = \frac{\tanh(\text{drive}\,x)}{\tanh(\text{drive})}

At zero drive, this expression has the identity mapping as its limit. The implementation uses a small-drive Taylor approximation to avoid division by zero while keeping both branches finite:

From synth/effects/distortion.py

safe_drive = drive.clamp_min(1e-4)
shaped = torch.tanh(safe_drive * audio) / torch.tanh(safe_drive)
near_zero = audio + drive.square() * (audio - audio.pow(3)) / 3
return torch.where(drive < 1e-4, near_zero, shaped)

Drive comes before the ADSR amplitude envelope. The final output is multiplied by both preset gain and MIDI velocity.

With all the components in place, the complete synthesizer is simply a sequence of operations transforming the MIDI input and preset parameters into an audio waveform.

The nice thing is that, from PyTorch's perspective, the entire synthesizer behaves much like any other differentiable function. We can compute a loss on its output and use automatic differentiation to obtain gradients with respect to its input parameters.

Of course, being differentiable doesn't necessarily mean that the parameters are easy to optimize. Different parameter combinations can produce very similar sounds, and the loss landscape can contain local minima. Even if gradients exist, they might not always point us toward the parameters we actually want.

So, before training any neural networks, the next question was: can we actually recover the synthesizer parameters by optimizing them directly?
