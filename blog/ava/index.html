Ava: Text-to-Synth

By Gianluigi Silvestri

Preamble

A few years ago, I got into music production as a hobby. I spent quite some time learning about sound design, although I never became particularly good at it. My usual workflow was something like this: I had a sound in mind, tried to recreate it on my favorite synthesizer, failed, and started scrolling endlessly through presets. I would find plenty of interesting sounds, but rarely the one I was looking for. Eventually, I would get distracted, forget my original idea, and give up.

Around the same time, I became curious about how synthesizers actually worked. I started reading The Audio Programmer's book on building synthesizer plugins, and realized that much of a synthesizer is essentially a sequence of mathematical operations on audio signals, many of which can be made differentiable.

That got me thinking: what if I implemented a synthesizer in PyTorch, and used backpropagation to learn its parameters?

The original idea was sound-to-synth: given an audio sample, a neural network would predict the synthesizer parameters needed to reproduce it. By passing these parameters through a differentiable synth and comparing the resulting audio with the original, we could train the network end-to-end, using the synthesizer itself as the decoder.

But then I realized this wasn't quite the problem I wanted to solve. How often do you have an audio sample of the exact sound you're imagining? Most of the time, you can at least try to describe it: a warm, slightly detuned pad with a slow attack, or a bright, punchy pluck. And even if the result isn't perfect, having a reasonably close preset to start tweaking would already make sound design much easier.

So the idea evolved into text-to-synth. I initially considered using a joint audio-text embedding model like CLAP, but eventually decided to take advantage of pretrained text-to-audio generative models to create paired text and audio data, and learn to map the resulting sounds to synthesizer parameters.

The result is Ava: a simple subtractive synthesizer that lets you generate presets from natural language descriptions, while still being able to play and tweak them like in any regular synth.

In this post, I'll walk through the synthesizer implementation, the training experiments, what worked (and what didn't), and how everything came together in a working plugin. Most of the project was developed on my MacBook M1 Pro, with a few hours of A100 GPU time for the more expensive experiments, and plenty of help from the open-source community.

And why Ava? It's named after the robot protagonist of Ex Machina, the movie that inspired me to study AI many years ago.
