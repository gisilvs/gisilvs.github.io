Ava: Text-to-Synth

By Gianluigi Silvestri

Preamble

A few years ago, I got into music production as a hobby. I spent quite some time learning about sound design, although I never became particularly good at it. My usual workflow was something like this: I had a sound in mind, tried to recreate it on my favorite synthesizer, failed, and started scrolling endlessly through presets. I would find plenty of interesting sounds, but rarely the one I was looking for. Eventually, I would get distracted, forget my original idea, and give up.

Around the same time, I became curious about how synthesizers actually worked. I started reading The Audio Programmer's book on building synthesizer plugins, and realized that much of a synthesizer is essentially a sequence of mathematical operations on audio signals, many of which can be made differentiable.

That got me thinking: what if I implemented a synthesizer in PyTorch, and used backpropagation to learn its parameters?

The original idea was sound-to-synth: given an audio sample, a neural network would predict the synthesizer parameters needed to reproduce it. By passing these parameters through a differentiable synth and comparing the resulting audio with the original, we could train the network end-to-end, using the synthesizer itself as the decoder.

But then I realized this wasn't quite the problem I wanted to solve. How often do you have an audio sample of the exact sound you're imagining? Most of the time, you can at least try to describe it: a warm, slightly detuned pad with a slow attack, or a bright, punchy pluck. And even if the result isn't perfect, having a reasonably close preset to start tweaking would already make sound design much easier.

So the idea evolved into text-to-synth. I initially considered using a joint audio-text embedding model like CLAP[1], but eventually decided to take advantage of pretrained text-to-audio generative models to create paired text and audio data, and learn to map the resulting sounds to synthesizer parameters.

The result is Ava: a simple subtractive synthesizer that lets you generate presets from natural language descriptions, while still being able to play and tweak them like in any regular synth.

In this post, I'll walk through the synthesizer implementation, the training experiments, what worked (and what didn't), and how everything came together in a working plugin. Most of the project was developed on my MacBook M1 Pro, with a few hours of A100 GPU time for the more expensive experiments, and plenty of help from the open-source community.

And why Ava? It's named after the robot protagonist of Ex Machina, the movie that inspired me to study AI many years ago.

The Synth

Let's start with the synthesizer itself. For this project, I wanted something relatively simple, but still expressive enough to generate a good variety of sounds. So I went with a subtractive synthesizer.

For those unfamiliar with sound synthesis, a subtractive synthesizer starts by generating one or more waveforms, typically rich in harmonics, and then shapes the resulting sound using filters, envelopes, and other modulations. It's one of the most common approaches to sound synthesis, and a good starting point for this project.

Ava follows a fairly standard architecture, illustrated in the diagram below.

The synth has two oscillators, each supporting four waveforms: sine, triangle, square, and sawtooth. Their outputs are combined using a mixing parameter, and the second oscillator can be detuned relative to the first to create richer sounds. White noise can also be blended into the mixed signal before it passes through a resonant low-pass filter, whose cutoff frequency can be modulated by a low-frequency oscillator (LFO). The signal then passes through drive, followed by an ADSR amplitude envelope and output gain.

This gives us a relatively small set of parameters to predict:

  • Oscillators: waveform selection for each oscillator, mix, and detuning.
  • Noise: white-noise mix.
  • Filter: cutoff frequency and resonance.
  • LFO: modulation rate and depth.
  • Envelope: attack, decay, sustain, and release.
  • Output: drive and gain.

One important design decision was to exclude pitch and velocity from the predicted parameters. Unlike the other parameters, which define a synth preset, pitch and velocity are generally determined by the incoming MIDI notes. Besides, estimating pitch from audio is already a challenging problem on its own, and I didn't want the model to have to solve both problems simultaneously.

Instead, the synthesizer receives pitch, velocity, and note-on/note-off events as separate MIDI inputs, while the neural network only predicts the preset parameters. This simplifies the learning problem, although it also means that we need to know the MIDI information associated with each training sample. We'll see later how I dealt with that.

The resulting synth is intentionally quite limited compared to commercial synthesizers. There are no effects like reverb or chorus, no complex modulation matrix, and only two oscillators. But that's also part of the experiment: how much can we do with a relatively simple synthesis engine, and how well can a neural network learn to control it?

Deep Dive: Building a Differentiable Synthesizer in PyTorchOptional · open to read

The main idea behind this project is that we want to use a synthesizer as a differentiable decoder. Given a set of parameters \theta and MIDI information m, the synthesizer produces an audio waveform:

\hat{x} = S(\theta, m)

We can then compare this waveform with target audio and backpropagate the loss through the synthesizer. The distinction is that \theta is an input to the synth, just like the MIDI information. The synthesizer itself is a fixed collection of signal-processing operations; it has no internal neural-network weights to learn.

For a single sound, we can make \theta a set of learnable parameters and optimize those values directly until the rendered audio matches the target. Alternatively, we can train a neural network to predict \theta from an audio representation. In that case, the network learns a mapping that we can reuse across many sounds, rather than solving a new optimization problem for each one.

Oscillators and waveform selection

Let's start with the oscillators. An oscillator generates a periodic waveform at a given frequency, determined by the incoming MIDI pitch. Ava supports four waveforms: sine, triangle, square, and sawtooth.

To generate audio, we evaluate a waveform at a sequence of evenly spaced times. In the equation below, t is time in seconds since note-on, and f is frequency in cycles per second (hertz):

x(t) = \sin(2\pi ft)

A sample rate of 24 kHz means taking 24,000 samples per second, so adjacent times are 1/24000 seconds apart. If f_s is the sample rate and N is the number of samples, the time vector is:

t_n = \frac{n}{f_s}, \qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1

The synth builds that vector once for the clip. Here, duration is in seconds and self.sample_rate is the number of samples per second:

From synth/synth.py, with explanatory comments

# Sample indices 0, 1, ..., N-1 become times in seconds after division.
time = torch.arange(round(duration * self.sample_rate),
                    device=freq.device, dtype=torch.float32) / self.sample_rate

Multiplying each time by freq tells us how many cycles have elapsed. Multiplying again by 2\pi converts cycles to an angle in radians. Finally, sine maps that angle to an amplitude between −1 and 1. At 200 Hz and 24 kHz, this takes 120 samples per cycle.

From synth/oscillators/sine.py, with explanatory comments

def __call__(self, freq, time):
    # freq: [batch, 1]; time: [samples].
    # Broadcasting evaluates every time point at each batch element's frequency.
    # 2*pi*freq*time is the phase in radians; initial phase is zero.
    return torch.sin(2 * torch.pi * freq * time)  # [batch, samples]

Every batch element can therefore have its own pitch, while sharing the same sampling times. For now there is no phase-offset control: all oscillators start at phase zero.

Square, triangle and sawtooth are built by adding sine waves at multiples of the fundamental frequency. These extra components are called harmonics. A square wave uses only odd harmonics: f,3f,5f,\ldots, with each amplitude divided by its harmonic number:

x_{\mathrm{square}}(t) \approx \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin(2\pi ft)+\frac{\sin(2\pi 3ft)}{3}+\frac{\sin(2\pi 5ft)}{5}+\cdots\right]

We start with the fundamental sine wave. Adding a smaller sine at three times its frequency begins to flatten the top and bottom. Further odd harmonics make the transitions steeper. The second component we add is therefore the third harmonic; the second harmonic, 2f, is absent.

Ava includes odd harmonics up to the 31st for square and triangle, and every harmonic up to the 31st for sawtooth. It also excludes any harmonic at or above half the sample rate, the Nyquist frequency. These are finite approximations: the small ripples in the square wave come from stopping the harmonic sum.

The square-wave code evaluates the harmonics together. Below, B is the batch size, N is the number of audio samples, and H = 16 is the number of odd harmonics from 1 to 31. Extra dimensions let PyTorch combine each frequency, harmonic and time without a Python loop:

From synth/oscillators/square.py, with explanatory comments

# Odd harmonic numbers: 1, 3, ..., 31. Initially [H].
harmonics = torch.arange(1, 32, 2, device=freq.device, dtype=freq.dtype)
harmonics = harmonics.view(1, -1, 1)  # [1, H, 1]
freq = freq.unsqueeze(1)             # [B, 1, 1]
time = time.unsqueeze(-2)            # [1, N], broadcast as [1, 1, N]
allowed = freq * harmonics < self.sample_rate / 2  # [B, H, 1]
# One sine wave per harmonic, evaluated at every sample time.
waves = torch.sin(2 * torch.pi * freq * harmonics * time)  # [B, H, N]
# Mask, weight by 1/harmonic, then sum H to produce [B, N].
return 4 / torch.pi * (allowed * waves / harmonics).sum(dim=1)

So far, pitch has come from MIDI and phase has been fixed at zero. We have not yet introduced a learnable preset control. The first one we want to learn is which oscillator waveform to use.

Waveform selection is a discrete operation: each oscillator must choose one of four possible waveforms. Unfortunately, a hard selection, such as taking the argmax of a neural network's output, is not differentiable.

One solution is to replace the hard selection with a softmax-weighted combination of all four waveforms:

x(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{4}p_k x_k(t), \qquad p_k=\operatorname{softmax}(z)_k

where z represents the predicted logits and x_k(t) the individual waveforms.

From synth/oscillators/bank.py

# Keep the four candidate waveforms on a separate axis: [batch, 4, samples].
waves = torch.stack([osc(freq, time) for osc in self.oscillators], dim=1)
# selection: [batch, 4] -> [batch, 4, 1], sharing each weight across time.
# Sum the waveform axis to return [batch, samples].
return (selection.unsqueeze(-1) * waves).sum(dim=1)

The bank's order is sine, square, sawtooth, triangle. Weights of [1, 0, 0, 0] therefore give a sine wave, while [0.5, 0.5, 0, 0] blend sine and square equally. This choice belongs to a single oscillator; the separate mix control later combines oscillator A with oscillator B.

This makes waveform selection differentiable, but introduces a mismatch: during training, the oscillator can mix multiple waveforms, whereas in the final plugin we want to select a single waveform.

An alternative is the straight-through estimator (STE), which uses a hard selection during the forward pass but approximates its gradients with those of the soft selection.

For one oscillator, the fitting code expresses this as:

# Turn four logits into probabilities, then make a one-hot hard choice.
probability = raw.softmax(dim=-1)
hard = torch.nn.functional.one_hot(probability.argmax(-1), 4).to(probability)
# The bracket is zero in the forward pass, but carries softmax gradients.
controls[name] = hard + (probability - probability.detach())

I experimented with both approaches, and found that soft selection was generally more stable, while the straight-through estimator made optimization considerably less reliable.

Detuning and mixing

Both oscillators begin with the MIDI note's frequency, but we can shift the second one slightly. Detuning is measured in cents: there are 100 cents in a semitone and 1,200 in an octave. Since moving up one octave doubles frequency, a shift of c cents multiplies frequency by 2^{c/1200}:

f_B = f_A\,2^{c/1200}

When we mix two close frequencies, their interference creates slow beating and a sense of movement, without changing the MIDI note being played.

The code first renders oscillator B at its shifted frequency, then crossfades between A and B. A mix of zero uses only A, one uses only B, and 0.5 gives equal contributions:

From synth/synth.py, with explanatory comments

# Convert the cents offset to a frequency ratio for oscillator B.
freq_b = freq * 2 ** (detune_cents / 1200)
wave_b = self.oscillators(freq_b, time, oscillator_b)
# wave is oscillator A here; mix controls their relative contribution.
wave = (1 - mix) * wave + mix * wave_b

After mixing the oscillators, a separate noise mix blends in white noise before the filter.

ADSR envelope

The oscillators give us a repeating waveform. An ADSR envelope gives the note its amplitude shape: attack raises the level from zero to one, decay lowers it toward sustain, sustain holds that level while the note is pressed, and release brings it back to zero after note-off. We multiply the audio by this envelope, sample by sample.

Attack, decay and release are durations in seconds; sustain is a level between zero and one. MIDI timing supplies when the note starts and stops. We optimize the envelope controls, while keeping those MIDI events as known inputs.

Given an attack duration in seconds, we need a function that rises from zero to one over that time. We use an exponential curve, stretched to match the attack duration:

From synth/envelopes/adsr.py, with explanatory comments

def curve(self, progress):
    # Before a stage starts, progress is 0; after it ends, progress is 1.
    progress = progress.clamp(0, 1)
    # self.curvature = 4.0. Normalize the exponential to run from 0 to 1.
    curve = -torch.expm1(-self.curvature * progress) / -math.expm1(-self.curvature)
    # Make completed transitions exactly 1, including in finite precision.
    return torch.where(progress >= 1, torch.ones_like(curve), curve)

The fixed curvature of 4 determines the bend: halfway through the stage, this curve has reached about 88% of its endpoint.

We use the same curve to rise from zero to one over the attack time, then fall from one to the sustain level over the decay time:

From synth/envelopes/adsr.py, with explanatory comments

def held_level(self, time, attack, decay, sustain):
    attack_level = self.curve(time / attack)  # Progress through attack.
    # Decay begins at time = attack, and drops by (1 - sustain).
    decay_level = 1 - (1 - sustain) * self.curve((time - attack) / decay)
    return torch.where(time < attack, attack_level, decay_level)

Release needs one more detail: the note might stop during attack or decay. We first evaluate the held-note envelope at the exact note-off time, then fade from that level to zero. This avoids jumping to the sustain level when the note is released early:

From synth/envelopes/adsr.py, with explanatory comments

# Amplitude while the key is held, evaluated at every audio sample.
held = self.held_level(time, attack, decay, sustain)
# Evaluate the same function at note-off, even if attack/decay is unfinished.
level_at_release = self.held_level(note_press_duration, attack, decay, sustain)
# Fade that level to zero over the release duration.
tail = level_at_release * (1 - self.curve((time - note_press_duration) / release))
return torch.where(time < note_press_duration, held, tail)

In these snippets, the controls have shape [batch, 1] and time has shape [samples], producing an envelope of shape [batch, samples]. Clip duration is separate from note duration: rendering six seconds does not mean holding the key for six seconds, and a clip can end before a long release has finished.

Low-pass filter and LFO modulation

The filter was probably the most interesting (and hardest) component to implement.

Ava uses a second-order resonant low-pass filter, also known as a biquad. A low-pass filter lets lower frequencies through and reduces higher ones. Its cutoff, measured in hertz, controls where it transitions between those two regions. Resonance, controlled by the value Q, emphasizes the frequencies around the cutoff.

A filter, one sample at a time

We already have an input waveform from the oscillators and noise. The filter turns it into a new waveform. To describe that process, we need a few symbols:

  • n is the sample index: 0, 1, 2, and so on. At 24 kHz, we advance by one sample every 1/24000 seconds.
  • x[n] is the input audio amplitude at sample n, before filtering.
  • y[n] is the output audio amplitude at that same sample, after filtering.
  • b_0,b_1,b_2 are weights applied to the current input sample and its two predecessors.
  • a_0 multiplies the current output; a_1,a_2 weight the two previous output samples.

In particular, x[n-1] means the input one sample ago, and y[n-2] means the output two samples ago. The subscripts on a and b label the weights; the terms in square brackets pick a moment in the audio.

The filter computes each output sample using this rule:

\begin{aligned} a_0 y[n] ={}& b_0 x[n] + b_1 x[n-1] + b_2 x[n-2]\\ &- a_1 y[n-1] - a_2 y[n-2] \end{aligned}

Read the first line as “take a weighted combination of the current input and the two preceding inputs.” Then subtract the weighted previous outputs on the second line. The coefficients themselves can be positive or negative; the minus signs are part of the way this filter is written.

Calculating and applying the filter

In Ava, the cutoff follows the played pitch to keep a similar effect across the keyboard. We express it as an octave offset from the note's frequency, then convert it to hertz before calculating the filter coefficients.

With the LFO switched off, cutoff and Q stay fixed throughout the note, so we only need one set of coefficients. For cutoff f_c, sample rate f_s and resonance Q, we first calculate:

\omega=\frac{2\pi f_c}{f_s},\qquad \alpha=\frac{\sin\omega}{2Q}

The low-pass coefficients are then:

\begin{aligned} b_0=b_2&=\frac{1-\cos\omega}{2}, & b_1&=1-\cos\omega\\ a_0&=1+\alpha, & a_1&=-2\cos\omega, & a_2&=1-\alpha \end{aligned}

The code below evaluates these expressions:

From synth/filters/lowpass.py, with explanatory comments

# Convert cutoff Hz to radians per sample.
omega = 2 * torch.pi * cutoff_hz / self.sample_rate
cosine = torch.cos(omega)
alpha = torch.sin(omega) / (2 * q)
# Pack [a0, a1, a2] and [b0, b1, b2] for each cutoff/Q pair.
a = torch.cat([1 + alpha, -2 * cosine, 1 - alpha], dim=-1)
b = torch.cat([(1 - cosine) / 2, 1 - cosine, (1 - cosine) / 2], dim=-1)
# Divide every coefficient by a0, so the new a0 is 1.
return a / a[:, :1], b / a[:, :1]

We can then pass the audio and coefficients to torchaudio.functional.lfilter, which handles the previous-input and previous-output terms for us:

From synth/filters/lowpass.py, with explanatory comments

# Compute one coefficient set per batch element from the fixed cutoff and Q.
batch = max(audio.shape[0], cutoff_hz.shape[0])
a, b = self.coefficients(cutoff_hz.expand(batch, 1), q.expand(batch, 1))
# lfilter evaluates the recurrence using these coefficients.
filtered = lfilter(audio.double().expand(batch, -1), a, b,
                   clamp=False, batching=True)
return filtered.to(audio.dtype)

Adding LFO modulation

The LFO moves the cutoff up and down. Its rate controls how fast it moves, and its depth controls how far. Each time the cutoff changes, we calculate new a and b coefficients using the same formulas above.

We update those coefficients at 200 Hz: every 5 milliseconds, or every 120 audio samples at 24 kHz. Between updates, the same coefficients remain in use while the filter continues producing audio at the full sample rate.

The following shows the operations for a single update. We start from the preset's base cutoff offset, add the LFO's contribution, convert to hertz and recalculate the coefficients. The full implementation evaluates the update times together in a batch.

Simplified from synth/synth.py and synth/filters/lowpass.py; batching omitted

# For one update time; the full implementation batches all update times.
offset = torch.as_tensor(cutoff_octaves, device=freq.device, dtype=torch.float64)
# Add the LFO's octave offset to the preset's base offset.
offset = offset + self.lfo(time, rate, depth)
cutoff_hz = freq.double() * 2 ** offset
# Recalculate a and b using exactly the same coefficient function as before.
a, b = self.lowpass.coefficients(cutoff_hz, q)

Drive, gain, and putting everything together

Drive adds soft distortion, reshaping the waveform and adding harmonics. For a drive amount d>0, an input sample x becomes:

y=\frac{\tanh(d\cdot x)}{\tanh(d)}

At zero drive, the audio is unchanged. Drive comes before the ADSR envelope; preset gain and MIDI velocity set the final output level.

With all the components in place, the complete synthesizer is simply a sequence of operations transforming the MIDI input and preset parameters into an audio waveform.

The nice thing is that, from PyTorch's perspective, the entire synthesizer behaves much like any other differentiable function. We can compute a loss on its output and use automatic differentiation to obtain gradients with respect to its input parameters.

Of course, being differentiable doesn't necessarily mean that the parameters are easy to optimize. Different parameter combinations can produce very similar sounds, and the loss landscape can contain local minima. Even if gradients exist, they might not always point us toward the parameters we actually want.

So, before training any neural networks, the next question was: can we actually recover the synthesizer parameters by optimizing them directly?

Is Differentiation Through the Synth Effective?

Now that we have a differentiable synthesizer, the first question is whether we can actually recover its parameters through gradient descent. Just because an operation is differentiable doesn't necessarily mean it's easy to optimize.

To test this, I started with a simple experiment. First, I chose a synthesizer preset and used it to generate target audio. Then, I randomly initialized the parameter I wanted to recover and optimized it by gradient descent to reproduce the target, keeping all other parameters fixed.

The objective was simply to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between the target and reconstructed waveforms:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(x_t-S(\theta,m)_t\right)^2

Here, x is the target audio, T is its number of samples, S is our synthesizer, \theta is the preset, and m is the MIDI input. Only the chosen entries of \theta are optimized; the rest stay at their ground-truth values.

Why use MSE for audio?

Waveform MSE is generally a poor measure of perceptual similarity. It's extremely sensitive to phase and temporal alignment: two signals that sound almost identical can have a large MSE if one is shifted by just a few samples. This is particularly problematic for audio generation, where we often care more about how a sound is perceived than exact waveform correspondence.

However, our setting is a little different. Both the target and reconstructed audio come from the same deterministic synthesizer, with identical MIDI inputs, note timing, oscillator starting phase and noise realization. This removes many sources of mismatch, and we know that a parameter configuration capable of producing the exact target waveform exists.

That doesn't make the optimization problem trivial. Changes in parameters such as oscillator detuning can still produce large phase differences, and the loss can contain multiple local minima. But MSE provides a simple and inexpensive starting point to test whether gradients through the synthesizer are useful.

Recovering the parameters

I first optimized each parameter individually, keeping the others fixed at their ground-truth values. I then repeated the experiment with multiple parameters optimized simultaneously, making the task progressively more difficult.

The results were encouraging: for the tested preset, every parameter was recovered in at least one run. That is a useful sanity check, rather than a guarantee across all possible sounds. The figure below shows three examples from the single-parameter tests.

Detuning and LFO rate were the main difficulties in the single-parameter tests. Detuning was recovered in two of the three seed groups, and LFO rate in one. All the other parameters were recovered in all three seed groups. These two can get stuck even when every other parameter is correct. A separate scan of the LFO-rate loss confirmed a local minimum near 1.708 Hz, away from the correct 2 Hz. Optimizing multiple parameters together adds further ambiguity, since changes in one parameter can be compensated by changes in another.

I also explored soft waveform selection and hard selection with a straight-through estimator. Soft selection lets the optimizer blend waveforms rather than immediately committing to one choice. I found soft selection much easier to fit, although the saved preset is evaluated with discrete oscillator choices.

What did we learn?

These experiments gave me confidence that the differentiable synthesizer was working as intended, but also highlighted an important limitation: a useful gradient doesn't guarantee that gradient descent will find the right parameters.

Parameter recovery and audio reconstruction are also different goals. Multiple presets can produce similar waveforms, so a low audio loss doesn't guarantee recovery of the original values. Both matter when interpreting these experiments.

Fortunately, our ultimate goal isn't to optimize a new set of parameters from scratch for every sound. Instead, we want to train a neural network that predicts them directly. By learning from many examples, it might provide a useful starting point without requiring a separate optimization for each sound.

Before moving to audio reconstruction losses, though, I wanted to answer a simpler question: can a neural network learn to recover the original synthesizer parameters directly from an audio representation?

Learning Synth Parameters from Audio Latents

To predict a preset from audio, we first need a useful representation of the sound. I chose a pretrained audio encoder: training one myself was beyond the scope and computational budget of this project, and learning from compact latents is much more feasible than working with raw waveforms. Some encoders also share their latent space with text-to-audio generators, which would give us a way to generate presets from text later.

But do these latents preserve the details needed to recover our synth parameters? To test this, I trained a network to predict the preset from an encoded sound, using sounds generated by Ava itself. That way, we know the original settings and can compare the predictions directly against them.

Data

For this experiment, we need recordings with known synthesizer settings. When I started the project in 2024, I considered NSynth[2], a dataset of individual instrument notes from Google's Magenta team. It includes pitch and velocity, but not the preset parameters we need. Many of its sounds also wouldn't necessarily be reproducible by Ava.

But then I realized something obvious: we already have a synthesizer, so why not generate the data ourselves? Sampling a preset and a MIDI note gives us both the audio and its ground-truth parameters.

I generated 100,000 training examples, 10,000 validation examples and 10,000 test examples, each with a new preset. Pitches were sampled uniformly from MIDI 21–78; velocity stayed fixed. Each clip lasts six seconds, with the key held for four seconds and two seconds left for the release tail.

The sampling isn't uniform for every control. Envelope times, resonance and LFO rate use log-uniform sampling; the other controls use linear-uniform sampling within chosen ranges. The cutoff range also leaves room for the LFO sweep. The 16 ordered oscillator pairs are balanced within each split.

From audio to latents

A six-second waveform at 24 kHz contains 144,000 samples. Rather than learn from those samples directly, I used a pretrained audio encoder to produce a shorter sequence for our prediction network.

I tried EnCodec[3] and Music2Latent[4] along the way, then settled on SAME-S, the autoencoder used by Stable Audio 3 Small SFX[5]. Those early runs weren't a controlled comparison of encoders. The practical attraction was that Stable Audio 3 can generate text-conditioned audio in the same latent space, giving us a route from text to our mapper later.

For these clips, SAME-S produces 66 frames of 256 values. The encoder stays frozen, and we save its latents alongside the audio, preset and MIDI information.

Parameter-Space Pretraining

With these labeled examples, we can start by predicting the original parameters directly. No audio needs to be rendered for the training updates, so this stage is much cheaper than learning through the synthesizer.

A small Transformer

I used a small Transformer with about 440,000 parameters. It maps the latent sequence to 13 continuous controls in [0,1] through sigmoid activations, and four logits for each oscillator. Softmax turns each set of logits into waveform probabilities; cross-entropy uses the logits directly.

The shared synth-control mapping then converts the continuous outputs to physical values such as seconds, hertz and cents, keeping that logic in the synthesizer.

All continuous controls receive equal weight in the regression loss. For the waveform labels, I average cross-entropy over the two heads and give that term a weight of 0.01:

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{param}}&=\frac{1}{13}\sum_{i=1}^{13}(\hat u_i-u_i)^2\\ \mathcal{L}&=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{param}}+0.01\,\frac{\mathrm{CE}_A+\mathrm{CE}_B}{2} \end{aligned}

Here, u and \hat u are the target and predicted normalized controls. Both loss terms are also averaged over the batch.

How well does it recover the parameters?

I trained for 200 epochs, using an exponential moving average of the weights for evaluation, and selected the checkpoint with the lowest validation parameter MSE. On the 10,000 test examples, the overall normalized parameter RMSE was 0.0488. Waveform accuracy was 92.7% for oscillator A, 93.2% for B, and 86.6% for getting both right.

LFO rate and decay remain the hardest controls, while release, gain, attack and cutoff are recovered more accurately. Detuning's error corresponds to about 1.37 cents. These results show that the encoder and mapper together recover useful information; they don't isolate whether remaining errors come from the representation, network capacity or ambiguity in the sounds. For example, a high filter cutoff can be difficult to infer when there is little high-frequency energy to remove.

The fourth example gets both waveforms right, but predicts an LFO rate of 4.68 Hz instead of 3.13 Hz. The preset comparisons make these differences easier to inspect alongside the audio.

Why not stop here?

Parameter supervision treats all normalized controls equally, but equal parameter errors don't imply equal changes in sound. It also ties us to examples with known presets. Sounds from an external text-to-audio model won't come with those labels, and may not be exactly reproducible by Ava.

The next step is therefore to train through the synthesizer: compare the audio produced by the predicted preset with the target sound, instead of comparing their parameter values.
